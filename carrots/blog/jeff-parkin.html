How Carrots Turned My Life Around After Being Sacked By Jeff FC

One Tuesday I was a football manager. By Wednesday I was a man sitting in his car outside a vegetable stall, crying into a Giant carrot. Two years later I get up at five every morning to wash mud off carrots, and I like it.

The sacking

I managed Jeff FC for six seasons. We went up once, came straight back down, and spent the rest of the time somewhere in the middle of the table. I wasn't the best manager the club ever had. I wasn't the worst either, though the board might argue with that.

Being called Jeff and managing a club called Jeff FC had its moments. Half the town thought the club was named after me. The other half thought I'd only got the job because of my name. When results went bad, the second half got louder.

The end came after a 4–0 loss away from home, our sixth defeat in a row. I'd changed the formation at half-time and we conceded three more. The chairman called me in on the Tuesday morning, told me the club was "going in a different direction", and handed me a bin bag with my tracksuit, a clipboard and a half-eaten Mars bar from my office drawer. I didn't argue. I'd seen the table.

"Mate, you look like a man who needs a carrot." That's the first thing Pete ever said to me.

The stall

I didn't go home. I drove up into the hills for an hour and ended up parked on a back road outside Olinda, next to a hand-painted sign that said WE GOT CARROTS. I hadn't eaten all day. Pete wandered over, looked at me through the window, and held out a carrot about the length of my forearm.

I don't know why, but I ate the whole thing sitting there with the engine off. It was the sweetest, crunchiest thing I'd had in months. For six years I'd lived on pies from the kiosk and whatever was left in the dressing room after the players had been through it. I'd forgotten food could taste of anything.

Pete didn't ask what was wrong. He asked if I could lift a crate. I said I used to carry the kit bags when the kitman was off sick. He said, "Good. Grab that end."

Working on the farm

In football everything is about the next result. A Humungous carrot takes most of a season in the ground. You water it, weed around it and leave it alone, and one day you pull it up and it's taller than your kid. Nobody calls for your head if it's slow. It took me a while to get used to that.

Half of what we grow comes out forked or twisted. Pete turns it into juice, soup and cake, and people queue for it on a Saturday. I spent six years trying to get the best out of players other clubs didn't want, so I've got a soft spot for the odd-shaped ones.

I used to lie awake on Friday nights going over team sheets. Now I'm out in the beds at sunrise with a flask of tea, the mist still sitting in the mountain ash, and the only noise is the magpies. On cold mornings up here that tea matters.

Some of the old habits stuck. I've got the Saturday stall organised like a match day, with a whiteboard in the shed and the juicing, the cake table and the till all set out before we open. Pete calls it my tactics board. He's not wrong.

Where I am now

I run the Saturday stall and look after the juicing. I've lost the weight I put on in the dugout, I sleep properly, and I can tell you the difference between a Big, a Giant and a Humungous from twenty metres away.

Some of my old players come by on their way to training now. They buy juice and tell me who's been dropped. The chairman came once, bought a carrot cake, and didn't make eye contact. I gave him an extra slice. No hard feelings.

Come and see us at the farm gate in Olinda on a Saturday. Ask for Jeff.

Jeff Parkin works Saturdays on the stall and is happy to talk about carrots, juicing or his six seasons in charge of Jeff FC. He would rather not talk about the half-time change of formation in his last game.
