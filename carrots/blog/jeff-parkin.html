Guest post by Jeff Parkin

How Carrots Turned My Life Around After Being Sacked By Jeff FC

One Tuesday I was a footballer. By Wednesday I was a man sitting in his car outside a vegetable stall, crying into a Giant carrot. Two years later I get up at five every morning to wash mud off carrots, and I like it.

The sacking

I played eleven seasons for Jeff FC. Mostly at left back, occasionally up front when we were desperate, once in goal when our keeper got stung by a wasp. I wasn't the best player we had. I wasn't the worst either, though the gaffer might argue with that.

Being called Jeff at a club called Jeff FC had its moments. Every time the crowd sang the club song I'd look up, thinking someone wanted me. Nobody ever did. Looking back, that should have been a sign.

The end came after a 4–0 loss away from home. I'd scored two of them. Into our own net. The gaffer called me in on the Tuesday morning, told me the club was "going in a different direction", and handed me a bin bag with my boots and a half-eaten Mars bar from my locker. I didn't argue. I'd seen the replays.

"Son, you look like a man who needs a carrot." That's the first thing Pete ever said to me.

The stall

I didn't go home. I drove up into the hills for an hour and ended up parked on a back road outside Olinda, next to a hand-painted sign that said WE GOT CARROTS. I hadn't eaten all day. Pete wandered over, looked at me through the window, and held out a carrot about the length of my forearm.

I don't know why, but I ate the whole thing sitting there with the engine off. It was the sweetest, crunchiest thing I'd had in months. At the club we were on protein shakes and boiled chicken. I'd forgotten food could taste of anything.

Pete didn't ask what was wrong. He asked if I could lift a crate. I said I'd been lifting weights three times a week for eleven years. He said, "Good. Grab that end."

Working on the farm

At Jeff FC everything was about the next ninety minutes. A Humungous carrot takes most of a season in the ground. You water it, weed around it and leave it alone, and one day you pull it up and it's taller than your kid. It took me a while to get used to waiting.

Half of what we grow comes out forked or twisted. Pete turns it into juice, soup and cake, and people queue for it on a Saturday. I was a fairly wonky left back myself. I'm a better juicer.

I used to dread 7am training. Now I'm out in the beds at sunrise with a flask of tea, the mist still sitting in the mountain ash, and the only noise is the magpies. On cold mornings up here that tea matters.

The legs still come in handy. Eleven years of running up and down the touchline means I can push a full barrow up the top paddock without stopping. Pete says I'm the best barrow man he's had. It's the nicest thing anyone's said about my legs.

Where I am now

I run the Saturday stall and look after the juicing. I've lost the bit of weight I put on after the sacking, I sleep properly, and I can tell you the difference between a Big, a Giant and a Humungous from twenty metres away.

Some of the lads from Jeff FC come by on their way to training now. They buy juice. The gaffer came once, bought a carrot cake, and didn't make eye contact. I gave him an extra slice. No hard feelings.

Come and see us at the farm gate in Olinda on a Saturday. Ask for Jeff.

Jeff Parkin works Saturdays on the stall and is happy to talk about carrots, juicing or left-back positioning. He would rather not talk about own goals.
